# Literature Review {#lit}

## Introduction

This chapter reviews the research that the study builds on. It first surveys the current state of the field: the divide between small and large language models, the evidence on what the capability gap consists of, retrieval as a substitute for scale, the compression of what a model reads, the reuse of a model's own keys and values, the benchmarks that evaluate memory in long interaction, and the security risks of persistent memory. It then examines specific related systems that implement memory for language models, identifies the gaps those systems leave, and closes with the conceptual framework that guided the study.

## The State of On-Device Language Models and External Memory

This section reviews the current state of the field: the rise of small on-device models, the composition of the capability gap, retrieval as a substitute for model scale, the compression and reuse of model inputs, and the evaluation and security of persistent memory.

### The Rise of On-Device Models

Scaling studies established that language model capability grows smoothly with parameter count and training data (Kaplan et al., 2020), which drove the field toward ever larger models served from the cloud. A counter-movement followed. Model families such as Qwen2.5 (A. Yang et al., 2024), Qwen3 (A. Yang et al., 2025), Llama 3.2 (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Phi-3 (Abdin et al., 2024) and Gemma 2 (Gemma Team, 2024) publish strong open-weight models at one to eight billion parameters, small enough to run in a few gigabytes of memory on an ordinary laptop. Apple has gone further and ships a roughly three-billion-parameter foundation model directly on its consumer devices, with a larger server model handling requests that exceed the on-device model's ability (Gunter et al., 2024). Apple's MLX framework runs such models on the integrated graphics processor of Apple silicon, in memory shared with the CPU (Hannun et al., 2023). A survey by Lu et al. (2024) documents both the speed of this progress and the consistent finding that small models remain clearly weaker than large ones, particularly on knowledge-intensive tasks.

### What the Capability Gap Is Made Of

The most important evidence for this study concerns the composition of the gap. Mallen et al. (2023) probed language models of many sizes on factual questions whose subjects ranged from very popular to very obscure, using a dataset they constructed for the purpose, PopQA. Three findings stand out. First, models of every size struggle on long-tail facts, and small models fail on them severely. Second, supplying retrieved text helps substantially on exactly those questions. Third, scaling the model up mainly improves recall of popular facts and barely improves the long tail at all. In other words, a large part of what users buy with model scale is memorised knowledge, and memorised knowledge is the one ingredient that can also be supplied from outside the model. Reasoning ability, by contrast, appears to be more deeply tied to scale, which is why this study treated knowledge-bound and reasoning-bound tasks separately and predicted different outcomes for each.

A long context window does not remove the need for memory. Liu et al. (2024) showed that models use information at the start and end of a long context far better than information in its middle, so text that is present is not necessarily used. Kuratov et al. (2024) built BABILong, which hides simple facts in book-length text, and found that models use only a fraction of their nominal window well. Both results suggest that what a model is given to read should be small and relevant, not merely long.

### Retrieval as a Substitute for Scale

A line of research spanning several years has demonstrated that letting a model read relevant external text at answer time can substitute for parameters. Retrieval-augmented generation showed that a model paired with a dense retriever over a document store outperforms much larger purely parametric models on knowledge-intensive tasks (Lewis et al., 2020), building on dense passage retrieval, which finds relevant text by comparing embedding vectors rather than keywords (Karpukhin et al., 2020). Borgeaud et al. (2022) made the substitution explicit: their RETRO model, augmented with retrieval from a trillion-token database, matched the performance of models with twenty-five times more parameters on language modelling benchmarks. Izacard et al. (2023) showed with Atlas that a retrieval-augmented model of eleven billion parameters could outperform a model fifty times its size on knowledge tasks. Together these results establish the premise this study depends on: parameters spent on memorising facts can, to a substantial degree, be replaced by retrieval, and the saving is largest precisely where small models are weakest. What these systems do not address is the on-device setting, where the retrieval corpus, the index and the model must all fit on a consumer machine and every token read costs measurable time.

### Compressing What the Model Reads

Every token a model reads costs time on a device, so the density of stored memory matters as much as its relevance. Research on prompt compression has shown that the text supplied to a model carries substantial redundancy. Jiang et al. (2023) demonstrated with LLMLingua that prompts can be compressed many times over, by removing low-information tokens, with little loss in the quality of the model's output. A complementary line compresses context into learned representations: Chevalier et al. (2023) taught models to summarise long contexts into compact vectors that stand in for the original text, and Ge et al. (2024) trained an in-context autoencoder that condenses a context window into a short sequence of memory slots. The lesson the baseline took was that memory should be stored densely, as short notes rather than verbatim transcripts, so that a fixed token budget carries the most useful information. Compression had so far been applied to prompts and contexts at inference time, not to the design of a persistent memory store, and never with the question of how it interacts with closing the capability gap on a device.

### Reusing the Model's Own Keys and Values {#kv-lit}

A transformer layer keeps, for every token it has read, a key and a value for each attention head; later tokens attend to them (Vaswani et al., 2017). This key–value cache is normally thrown away when a request ends, and building it is most of the cost of reading a long prompt. Several lines of work keep it instead. Transformer-XL carried the previous segment's hidden states into the next so that a model could attend beyond its segment (Dai et al., 2019). Memorizing Transformers stored past keys and values in an external memory and let one layer attend to the nearest of them, improving language modelling over long documents (Y. Wu et al., 2022). Serving systems page the cache in blocks so that many requests share memory efficiently (Kwon et al., 2023). Prompt Cache reuses the attention states of prompt modules that recur across requests (Gim et al., 2024), and CacheBlend combines the caches of separately processed retrieved passages, recomputing a small share of tokens so that the passages can attend to each other (Yao et al., 2025).

Two facts from this work shaped the engine. First, a saved cache is exact: laid back in front of the model, it gives the same result as reading the text again, at a fraction of the time. Second, caches made separately do not combine for free, because passages written apart never attended to each other; this is the problem CacheBlend works around. Keys also record each token's position as a rotation (Su et al., 2024), so a cache moved to a new position must be turned. None of these systems uses a saved cache as a persistent personal memory on a laptop, and none measures what such a memory costs in storage as it grows.

### Evaluation and Risks of Persistent Memory

Benchmarks for conversational memory have matured recently and informed this study's evaluation design. Maharana et al. (2024) introduced LoCoMo, a benchmark of very long multi-session conversations, and found that models, even with long context windows or retrieval, lag far behind human recall of conversation history. D. Wu et al. (2024) introduced LongMemEval, five hundred questions embedded in long user–assistant histories testing information extraction, multi-session reasoning, temporal reasoning, knowledge updates and abstention, and found that commercial assistants suffer accuracy drops of around thirty percent when information must be remembered across sustained interaction. They also decompose memory design into indexing, retrieval and reading stages, a decomposition the engine mirrors in its write path, read path and budget. These benchmarks establish that memory in long interaction is an unsolved problem even for large cloud systems; neither, however, measures memory as an instrument for closing the small-versus-large capability gap, which is this study's concern.

A memory store that accepts writes from interaction is also an attack surface, and recent security research documents the risk concretely. Chen et al. (2024) showed with AgentPoison that an agent's long-term memory or retrieval store can be poisoned with a small number of crafted records, so that queries containing an optimised trigger retrieve the malicious records and steer the agent's behaviour while benign queries are left untouched. Dong et al. (2025) went further with MINJA, demonstrating that an attacker with ordinary query access, and no privileged write access at all, can inject malicious records into an agent's memory purely through crafted interactions. These findings motivated two design choices: every stored memory carries its source and date, and the baseline screened each note before storing it. A full security evaluation was beyond the study's scope.

## Related Works

This section examines specific systems that implement memory for language models, from operating-system-inspired context management to brain-inspired associative retrieval and production memory layers. Table {tab:related} summarises the comparison.

### MemGPT

Packer et al. (2023) framed the memory problem in operating-system terms. The model's limited context window is treated as fast main memory, an external store as disk, and the system pages information in and out as the conversation demands, allowing a fixed-context model to sustain interactions far longer than its window. The framing is influential because it makes explicit that what the model reads at each step is a policy decision, not a given, which is exactly the decision this study's budget controls; the engine's movement of rooms between RAM and the SSD follows the same idea at the level of saved tensors. MemGPT manages context length, however; it does not target capability gain, and it does not account for token cost or device latency. Figure {fig:memgpt} illustrates the two-tier architecture.

### Generative Agents

Park et al. (2023) gave simulated agents a memory stream of timestamped observations and retrieved from it by combining three scores: relevance to the current situation, recency and importance. Their generative agents produced believable long-running behaviour in a simulated town, and their three-part retrieval score was adopted by the baseline's retrieval policy. The system was designed for simulation rather than question answering, and its memory records are verbose natural-language entries, which makes it an instructive design source but not an answer to the efficiency questions a device imposes. Figure {fig:smallville} shows the simulated town in which the agents live, work and remember.

![{#smallville} The Smallville environment of Generative Agents (Source: Park et al., 2023, project repository)](figures/ch2/fig2_2.png){width=14.5}

### MemoryBank

Zhong et al. (2024) added a model of forgetting. MemoryBank strengthens or decays stored memories over time in the spirit of the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve, so that what the system retains reflects both how often a memory is used and how old it is. The contribution is a principled answer to what should be kept, complementing the question of what should be retrieved; the engine's vault applies the same idea, letting a note's strength fade each day it is unused. Like the systems above, MemoryBank is evaluated on the quality of long-term companionship behaviour rather than on capability gain, token cost or latency. Figure {fig:memorybank} reproduces the framework figure from the project's repository.

![{#memorybank} The MemoryBank framework with memory updating and forgetting (Source: Zhong et al., 2024, project repository)](figures/ch2/fig2_3.png){width=14.5}

### HippoRAG

Plain similarity search retrieves passages that resemble the question, but some questions require connecting facts that do not resemble the question at all. Cognitive science offers a model for this in spreading activation, where recalling one concept activates linked concepts (Collins & Loftus, 1975), and in the hippocampal indexing theory, where a compact index links the distributed traces of an experience so that touching one part can reactivate the whole (Teyler & DiScenna, 1986). Gutierrez et al. (2024) operationalised these ideas in HippoRAG: a language model extracts entities from stored passages into a knowledge graph that acts as the index, and at question time the entities in the query seed a Personalized PageRank walk over the graph, spreading relevance to connected passages, including multi-hop neighbours that similarity search misses. Figure {fig:hipporag} illustrates the pipeline. HippoRAG outperformed strong retrieval baselines on multi-hop question answering by up to twenty percent while being many times cheaper and faster than iterative retrieval methods. Its relevance to this study is twofold: it demonstrates the value of associative structure in memory, and its computational profile, graph construction by a language model and a graph walk per query, illustrates the kind of cost that must be justified against a device budget. The engine's rooms are a lighter version of the same index: a compact grouping of related moments that one question reactivates together.

![{#hipporag} HippoRAG offline indexing and online retrieval (Source: Gutierrez et al., 2024, project repository)](figures/ch2/fig2_4.png){width=14.5}

### Mem0

Production systems have begun packaging these ideas. Mem0 extracts and consolidates salient facts from conversations into a managed store and reports large token savings relative to feeding a model its full history (Chhikara et al., 2025). Its existence is evidence that the engineering direction is commercially credible. As a cloud service, however, it does not answer the on-device question, and like the research systems it does not measure gap closing per task type. Figure {fig:mem0}, drawn from the project's documentation, shows where the memory layer sits in an agent stack, reading at question time and writing after each response.

## Gaps in Related Works {#gaps}

The reviewed literature establishes each ingredient separately. Retrieval can substitute for model scale on knowledge-heavy tasks (Lewis et al., 2020; Borgeaud et al., 2022; Izacard et al., 2023), and it helps most on exactly the long-tail knowledge where small models fail (Mallen et al., 2023). Memory systems can accumulate, organise, score and forget information across long interactions (Packer et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024; Gutierrez et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025). Text supplied to a model can be heavily compressed with little loss (Jiang et al., 2023; Chevalier et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2024), and a model's own keys and values can be saved and reused (Y. Wu et al., 2022; Gim et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025). Benchmarks exist that expose how poorly current systems remember long interactions (Maharana et al., 2024; D. Wu et al., 2024; Kuratov et al., 2024).

What was missing is the combination and the measurement. No existing work assembles these ingredients into a memory engine beside a frozen small model on a consumer device and then answers the deployment question directly: what fraction of the small-versus-large capability gap does the engine close, separately for knowledge-bound and reasoning-bound tasks, and at what cost in time, RAM and storage on the device as the memory grows past any context window? Existing memory systems are evaluated on recall or task success, not on gap closing; key–value reuse is evaluated as a serving optimisation, not as a persistent memory; and no study reports honest per-answer cost accounting on consumer hardware. This intersection defines the gap this study addressed.

## Conceptual Framework {#framework}

Figure {fig:framework} presents the conceptual framework that structured the study, as it was set out at the proposal stage. The independent variable is the configuration of the memory engine: how what the user tells it is kept (the write policy), how a question finds what it needs (the retrieval policy), and how much memory the model reads (the budget). The process is question answering on the device: the engine recalls relevant memory, assembles it within the budget, and the frozen model generates an answer from the question and the assembled memory. The dependent variables are the share of the capability gap closed, the accuracy gained per token of memory supplied, and the end-to-end latency on the device. Task type, knowledge-bound against reasoning-bound, moderates the relationship: substantial gap closing was predicted on knowledge-bound questions and little on reasoning-bound ones. The language model itself is held constant throughout, weights frozen, so that every measured gain is attributable to the memory engine alone.

![{#framework} Conceptual framework of the study](figures/ch2/fig2_6.png){width=15.9}

The framework held for the engine as built, with one change in what each variable means. In the final engine the write policy keeps a moment as the model's notes in a room, the retrieval policy chooses one room and the closest moments within a reach, and the budget is the size of a room plus the moments re-read. The dependent variables gained two measures that matter once memory grows: the RAM of the whole process and the storage the memory takes on the SSD.

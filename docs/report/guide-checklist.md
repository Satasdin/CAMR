# Guide checklist for the final report

Source: `Research Writing for CS 2 Projects - 2026 (1).pdf` (194 pages), read in full on 10 November 2026, with the pictures on pages 37, 51, 54, 59, 72 to 74, 106 and 107 viewed as images. Page numbers in brackets are the guide's. Where the guide and the 2025 reports differ, the guide wins. Each line is ticked in the last section of this file once the Word document has been checked against it.

## Whole document

- W1. Third person. Past tense for completed work, present tense for established knowledge and for what the document, figures and tables show, future tense only for work not yet done (63, 80).
- W2. Objectives stay in the infinitive, and the report says how each was achieved (63 to 64).
- W3. Abstract revised last so that it reflects the real methods, results and conclusion (64).
- W4. APA 7th referencing generated by a reference manager (Mendeley or Zotero). No "n.d." sources, no Wikipedia, mostly peer-reviewed papers, books and official publications (60).
- W5. Class template used: cover page with title, name, admission number, ICS group, supervisor, month and year, and the project type "[Internal Project]" under the title (105 to 107). Declaration signed by student and supervisor, never invented (108, 192).
- W6. Front matter lists: Table of Contents, List of Figures, List of Tables, List of Equations, List of Abbreviations (1, 192).
- W7. Subtitle numbering follows the template's numbering (44).
- W8. Every figure and table numbered and captioned, cross-referenced in the text, readable at normal document size (72, 95).
- W9. Appendices numbered "Appendix 1, Appendix 2", each titled. Gantt chart to the end of November 2026. Extra diagrams cross-referenced from the body. One-page similarity report attached last (60 to 61, 108).
- W10. A feedback-resolution matrix: issue, source, affected section, action, status (63).
- W11. An AI-use log and disclosure of material AI use. Nothing fabricated: no invented citations, data, participants, results, screenshots or approvals (64, 76).
- W12. File name `StudentName_AdmissionNumber_SupervisorSurname` (108).
- W13. Alignment kept from problem and gap through objectives, questions, literature, methodology, implementation, testing and evaluation (63, 192).

## Front matter

- F1. Acknowledgements: formal, only people who played a role in the research (7).
- F2. Abstract: 100 to 250 words, most about 200. Background, objective, method, findings with the result, conclusion on what it means. Three paragraphs as in the example (7 to 10).
- F3. Keywords drawn from the abstract, in bold italics (10).

## Chapter 1: Introduction

- 1.1 Background Information: history and overview of the field, the gap, why the gap matters, the rationale. A gap-driven story, heavily cited but concise, no detour into a literature review (12 to 16).
- 1.2 Problem Statement: about half a page or 250 words. The ideal, the reality, the consequences and this study's role. Evidence cited, the problem quantified where possible (16 to 17).
- 1.3 Research Objectives: action-oriented and measurable. 1.3.1 General Objective in one sentence, "To design/develop...". 1.3.2 Specific Objectives in small roman numerals: (i) analyse the current state and its challenges, (ii) study how existing works have addressed it, (iii) review the gaps, (iv) design and develop, (v) test and validate (17 to 19). The approved proposal's wording is kept word for word.
- 1.4 Research Questions: derived one for one from the objectives (19 to 20).
- 1.5 Justification: what is known, what is missing, the problem this causes, how this study helps, and a closing sentence on who benefits (20 to 21).
- 1.6 Scope: technical, data, functional and time scope, and exclusions (21 to 22).
- 1.7 Limitations and Delimitations: limitations are outside the study's control, delimitations are chosen boundaries, each stated plainly (22 to 24).

## Chapter 2: Literature Review

- 2.1 Introduction: restates the problem, lays out the chapter, names the kinds of sources and the inclusion criteria (sources three to five years old where possible), previews each section (27 to 28).
- 2.2 Current State of the field, tied to objective i: an overview subsection (definition, current approaches, what works, what does not) and a last subsection "Key Challenges" in prose with citations (28 to 29).
- 2.3 Related Works, tied to objective ii: one subsection per system with its description, its technology and results, and its gaps. A comparative table of systems, strengths and gaps (30 to 32).
- 2.4 Gaps in Related Works: prose, cited, referring back to the related works and cross-referencing the table. Ends with the unmet need this study targets (32 to 34).
- 2.5 Conceptual Framework (34 to 38): definition and purpose; key variables as input, processing and output; theoretical foundation; a visual representation as a flow of the system (person and question in, named processing stages, outputs, feedback loop, as on page 37); relationships between variables; flexibility and refinement; a narrative of one question passing through; alignment with the design and test objectives.

## Chapter 3: Methodology

- 3.1 Introduction: short, frames the chapter, and states the development paradigm (OOAD or SSAD) (38 to 39). For AI projects it introduces the experimental design (39 to 40).
- 3.2 Research Paradigm, in prose: experimental paradigm, measurable objective, data, evaluation (40).
  - 3.2.1 Data Acquisition: sources, what a record contains, an example of the question and gold answer pairs, privacy compliance (40 to 42).
  - 3.2.2 Data Processing: cleaning, transformation, indexing for retrieval (42).
  - 3.2.3 Model Training: the model and how it is trained or, here, why it is not trained and what is set instead (43).
  - 3.2.4 Model Validation and Testing: metrics, held-out test data that never appears in development, how results are judged (43 to 44).
- 3.3 System Development Methodology: the methodology named and tied to the paradigm (OOAD suits iterative and incremental methods) (44 to 49). 3.3.1 Justification of the Methodology, supported by cited literature (49 to 50).
- 3.4 Methodology Diagram: one diagram, its source cited if it is not the student's own, then a discussion of each stage and how it was applied to this project. The stages cover planning, design with the paradigm named, the tools, iteration, testing (unit, integration, system, black box and white box, performance measures) and final evaluation (50 to 58).
- 3.5 System Analysis Diagrams and 3.6 System Design Diagrams: the diagrams chosen from the OOAD column of the table on page 59 (use case, sequence, activity, class; database schema, wireframes, system architecture), cross-referenced to Chapter 4 (58 to 59).
- 3.7 Deliverables: the outputs of the project (59).
- 3.8 Tools and Techniques: each tool listed and justified (59).

## Chapter 4: System Analysis and Design

- 4.1 Introduction: concise, outlines the sections and the diagrams, restates the paradigm (65).
- 4.2 System Requirements, opened with a sentence such as "The system requirements identified for this project are presented in the following subsections." Each requirement has a unique identifier, one statement, no vague words without a measure, and a way to verify it. No invented thresholds (65 to 66).
  - 4.2.1 Functional Requirements (FR-01...) (66 to 67).
  - 4.2.2 Non-Functional Requirements (NFR-01...), measurable and specific to the project (67 to 68).
  - 4.2.3 Data Requirements (DR-01...): inputs, outputs, formats, quality, storage, privacy, test separation (68 to 69).
  - 4.2.4 Interface and Integration Requirements (IR-01...): user interface, APIs, protocols, behaviour when an integration fails (69 to 70).
  - Traceability from each requirement back to an objective and forward to a feature, a diagram and a test (70).
- 4.3 System Analysis Diagrams and 4.4 System Design Diagrams. For every diagram: a narrative before it, the diagram in correct notation, a numbered caption, and an interpretation after it that explains the flows and decisions instead of repeating labels. Labels readable at normal size. Names consistent across diagrams (71 to 74).
- Use case diagram: actors outside the boundary, verb-based use cases, no databases or code modules as actors (72 to 73).
- Database: a logical schema with entities, keys, relationships and cardinalities; a relational schema is not forced onto a project that does not have one (73 to 74).
- AI-assisted diagrams are verified against the requirements and recorded in the AI-use log (75 to 76).

## Chapter 5: System Implementation, Testing and Evaluation

- 5.1 Introduction: what was built, how it was tested and evaluated. Ends with results, discussion and limitations (conclusions belong to a later chapter) (79).
- Past tense, third person, claims supported by traceable evidence. Screenshots support the text and are not proof by themselves (80).
- 5.2 Implementation Environment: development and test environment told apart, nothing called public that is not (80).
  - 5.2.1 Hardware and Deployment Environment: a compact table of item, tested specification, purpose and justification, evidence or source (80 to 81).
  - 5.2.2 Software, Services and Configuration: exact tested versions, purpose and reason. No secrets shown (82 to 83).
  - 5.2.3 Deployment and Reproducibility: how the solution is installed, configured, started and verified, with assumptions and paid services stated (83).
- AI and machine learning route (86 to 89): Dataset Description (name, source, why it suits, what one record contains, size, licence, a captioned sample); Data Preparation and Partitioning (how leakage was prevented, what is held out); Model Training and Selection (baseline and candidates, versions, settings, seeds; for retrieval-augmented systems the passage preparation, indexing, retrieval and prompting, and model training reported only if it happened); Model Evaluation and Application Testing (measures, correct and wrong examples, the model at work in the application, valid and out-of-scope questions, response time).
- 5.3 System Implementation in the order of the Chapter 4 architecture. Each component linked to its requirement, with evidence that it works. Deviations from the design recorded and justified. No code listings (84).
- Testing and Evaluation (92 to 95): test strategy and acceptance criteria stated before results (levels: unit, integration, system; positive, negative, boundary and exception cases; each test mapped to a requirement). Test cases in a table: Test ID, traceability, scenario and data, expected result, actual result with evidence and verdict. Failed tests and defects reported, not hidden. AI evaluation: a meaningful baseline, task-appropriate metrics, subgroup results, error analysis, final held-out performance. Results in tables for precise comparison and figures for patterns, with conditions, sample size and repetitions stated. No generalising beyond what was evaluated.
- Version Control and Implementation Evidence: repository link on a readable label, README, meaningful commits, tags, how the workflow supported the work (95 to 96).
- Author Oversight and Use of AI-Assisted Tools: a short factual declaration naming the tool and provider, what it assisted with, how output was checked, and that the student remains responsible (97).
- Evaluation Results, Discussion and Limitations: organised around the research objectives and questions. For each: the output, the evidence, the criterion, the observed result, and what it permits the study to conclude. Compared with the baseline and the Chapter 2 literature. Limitations stated honestly, with their likely effect (97 to 98).

## Machine learning notes that apply to this project (113 to 151)

- M1. A baseline is a reference method for comparison, and it is named (132, 148).
- M2. Training, validation and test roles are kept apart. Tuning prompts or thresholds against the final test questions is test-set tuning even when no weights change (126 to 128).
- M3. Leakage: the memory is never built from the questions or their answers. What is unseen is stated exactly (130).
- M4. Retrieval is separated from answering: did the right passage reach the model, and did the model use it (133, 143).
- M5. Question answering evaluation: scoring rules stated, counts as well as percentages, unanswerable and out-of-scope behaviour checked, the decision to answer evaluated apart from correctness (142 to 143).
- M6. Synthetic data is declared: generator, checks, and its limits (136 to 138).
- M7. Cost and reliability: response time on the intended hardware, same questions and conditions for every model, small differences on small test sets treated with care, repeated runs where feasible (144).
- M8. Evidence to prepare: problem and baseline, data provenance and counts, split design, pipeline design, model versions and settings, controlled comparisons, task metrics with errors, limitations and oversight (148).

## Further notes on the build phase that apply (152 to 193)

- B1. Traceability record connecting each objective or requirement to its component, test, result and evidence, with any unmet requirement explained (159).
- B2. Chapter 4 diagrams updated to match what was built, so that the diagram, code, interface and report describe the same system (161).
- B3. Interface for an AI system: the system state is visible, retrieved evidence is told apart from generated text, sources are traceable, uncertainty is not invented (163 to 164).
- B4. Test record for each test: requirement, input, expected, actual, verdict, evidence, correction and retest (191).
- B5. Reproducible handover: repository and release tag, setup and run instructions, known limitations, a user guide (192).
- B6. Whole-report review: abstract, contents, lists, numbering, captions, cross-references, APA 7 citations and reference list all checked (192).

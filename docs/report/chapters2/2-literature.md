# Chapter 2:  Literature Review

## 2.1 Introduction

This chapter reviews the research that the study builds on. It first surveys the current state of the field: the divide between small and large language models, the evidence on what the capability gap consists of, retrieval as a substitute for scale, the compression of what a model reads, the benchmarks that evaluate memory in long interaction, and the security risks of persistent memory. It then examines specific related systems that implement memory for language models, together with the systems that Burrow is compared with or built to work beside, identifies the gaps those systems leave, and closes with the conceptual framework that guided the study.

## 2.2 On-Device Language Models and External Memory

This section reviews the current state of the field: the rise of small on-device models, the composition of the capability gap, retrieval as a substitute for model scale, the compression of model inputs, and the evaluation and security of persistent memory.

### 2.2.1 The Rise of On-Device Models

Scaling studies established that language model capability grows smoothly with parameter count and training data (Kaplan et al., 2020), which drove the field toward ever larger models served from the cloud. A counter-movement followed. Model families such as Qwen2.5 (Yang et al., 2024), Llama 3.2 (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Phi-3 (Abdin et al., 2024), and Gemma 2 (Gemma Team, 2024) published strong open-weight models at one to three billion parameters, small enough to run in a few gigabytes of memory on an ordinary laptop. Apple has gone further and ships a roughly three billion parameter foundation model directly on its consumer devices, with a larger server model handling requests that exceed the on-device model's ability (Gunter et al., 2024). The families released in 2026, Gemma 4 (Gemma Team, 2026) and Qwen3.5 (Qwen Team, 2026), include the two models hosted in this study, at twelve and four billion parameters. A survey by Lu et al. (2024) documents both the speed of this progress and the consistent finding that small models remain clearly weaker than large ones, particularly on knowledge-intensive tasks.

### 2.2.2 Capability Gaps in Processing

The most important evidence for this study concerns the composition of the gap. Mallen et al. (2023) probed language models of many sizes on factual questions whose subjects ranged from very popular to very obscure, using a dataset they constructed for the purpose, PopQA. Three findings stand out. First, models of every size struggle on long-tail facts, and small models fail on them severely. Second, supplying retrieved text helps substantially on exactly those questions. Third, and most strikingly, scaling the model up mainly improves recall of popular facts and barely improves the long tail at all. In other words, a large part of what users buy with model scale is memorised knowledge, and memorised knowledge is the one ingredient that can also be supplied from outside the model. Reasoning ability, by contrast, appears to be more deeply tied to scale, which is why this study treated knowledge-bound and reasoning-bound tasks separately and predicted different outcomes for each.

### 2.2.3 The Scalability-Accuracy Trade-Off

A line of research spanning several years has demonstrated that letting a model read relevant external text at answer time can substitute for parameters. Retrieval-augmented generation showed that a model paired with a dense retriever over a document store outperforms much larger purely parametric models on knowledge-intensive tasks (Lewis et al., 2020), building on dense passage retrieval techniques that find relevant text by comparing embedding vectors rather than keywords (Karpukhin et al., 2020). Borgeaud et al. (2022) made the substitution explicit: their RETRO model, augmented with retrieval from a trillion-token database, matched the performance of models with twenty-five times more parameters on language modelling benchmarks. Izacard et al. (2023) showed with Atlas that a retrieval-augmented model of eleven billion parameters could outperform a model fifty times its size on knowledge tasks. Together these results establish the premise this study depends on: parameters spent on memorising facts can, to a substantial degree, be replaced by retrieval, and the saving is largest precisely where small models are weakest. What these systems do not address is the on-device setting, where the retrieval corpus, the index, and the model must all fit on a consumer machine and every token read costs measurable time.

### 2.2.4 Token-Efficient Retrieval

Every token a model reads costs time on a device, so the density of what is supplied matters as much as its relevance. Research on prompt compression has shown that the text supplied to a model carries substantial redundancy. Jiang et al. (2023) demonstrated with LLMLingua that prompts can be compressed many times over, by removing low-information tokens, with little loss in the quality of the model's output. A complementary line compresses context into learned representations: Chevalier et al. (2023) taught models to summarise long contexts into compact vectors that stand in for the original text, and Ge et al. (2024) trained an in-context autoencoder that condenses a context window into a short sequence of memory slots. The lesson this study takes is that what the model reads should be dense, short excerpts and short notes rather than whole documents or verbatim transcripts, so that a fixed token budget carries the maximum useful information. Compression has so far been applied to prompts and contexts at inference time, not to the design of a persistent memory, and never with the question of how the amount read interacts with capability gap closing on a device.

### 2.2.5 Evaluation and Risks of Persistent Memory

Benchmarks for conversational memory have matured recently and informed this study's evaluation design. Maharana et al. (2024) introduced LoCoMo, a benchmark of very long multi-session conversations, and found that models, even with long context windows or retrieval, lag far behind human recall of conversation history. Wu et al. (2025) introduced LongMemEval, five hundred questions embedded in long user-assistant histories testing information extraction, multi-session reasoning, temporal reasoning, knowledge updates, and abstention, and found that commercial assistants suffer accuracy drops of around thirty percent when information must be remembered across sustained interaction. They also decompose memory design into indexing, retrieval, and reading stages, a decomposition Burrow mirrors in its index, its retrieval, and its token budgeting. These benchmarks establish that memory in long interaction is an unsolved problem even for large cloud systems; neither, however, measures memory as an instrument for closing the small-versus-large capability gap, which is this study's concern.

A memory store that accepts writes from interaction is also an attack surface, and recent security research documents the risk concretely. Chen et al. (2024) showed with AgentPoison that an agent's long-term memory or retrieval store can be poisoned with a small number of crafted records so that queries containing an optimised trigger retrieve the malicious records and steer the agent's behaviour, with benign queries left untouched. Dong et al. (2025) went further with MINJA, demonstrating that an attacker with ordinary query access, and no privileged write access at all, can inject malicious records into an agent's memory purely through crafted interactions. These findings motivated two design choices in Burrow: every note the engine proposes is backed by the person's own words, and nothing is written to the vault without the person's consent. A full security evaluation is beyond the study's scope, but designing the memory so that such an evaluation is possible later costs little and reflects sound practice.

## 2.3 Memory, Retrieval, and Compression Techniques

The preceding section established the capability gap and the principle that external knowledge can substitute for parameters. This section addresses the first objective of the study directly by surveying the techniques that make that substitution work in practice: how memory is retrieved and how the text supplied to a model is compressed. The review is organised by the scale of the host model, from large cloud language models, to small language models, to fully on-device deployment, because the constraints, and therefore the appropriate techniques, change sharply as the model shrinks and moves onto the device.

### 2.3.1 Techniques for Large Language Models (LLMs)

At the scale of large cloud models, retrieval and compression are optimisation choices rather than necessities, and the literature has accordingly focused on maximising answer quality. Retrieval-augmented generation pairs a model with a dense retriever so that relevant passages are read at answer time (Lewis et al., 2020), and subsequent work showed that retrieval can stand in for a large fraction of model scale on knowledge-intensive tasks (Borgeaud et al., 2022; Izacard et al., 2023). On the compression side, prompt-compression methods such as LLMLingua demonstrated that the context fed to a large model carries substantial redundancy that can be removed with little loss of accuracy (Jiang et al., 2023), and learned context-compression approaches encode long contexts into compact representations that stand in for the original text (Chevalier et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2024). A recent advance relevant to this study is personalised, behaviour-aware compression: rather than compressing raw demonstrations, the personalised knowledge refinement framework analyses a target model's own errors and distils them into a compact, model-specific instruction context, reducing context length and inference latency while improving task accuracy (Wang et al., 2026). These techniques set the conceptual ceiling for what memory and compression can achieve, but they assume cloud-scale compute and do not account for the memory, energy, and latency budgets of a device.

### 2.3.2 Techniques for Small Language Models (SLMs)

As models shrink to the small language model range, typically a few hundred million to a few billion parameters, retrieval and compression shift from optional to essential, because the model can no longer rely on parametric knowledge alone. A recent survey of on-device small language models organises the field into a co-design stack spanning compact architecture, data-centric training, post-training compression, parameter-efficient adaptation, and inference-time systems, and identifies key-value cache memory and quantisation brittleness as the dominant constraints (Patra et al., 2026). Two compression families recur throughout this literature. Knowledge distillation trains a small student to imitate a larger teacher, and has been shown to transfer most of the teacher's task performance into a model small enough for edge deployment (Renuka et al., 2025). Quantisation reduces the numerical precision of weights and activations, cutting memory and accelerating inference, and is now a default step before deployment, though it interacts with retrieval through the growth of the key-value cache on long contexts (Patra et al., 2026). Critically for this study, recent evaluation shows that small instruction-tuned models, augmented with lightweight calibration and modest supervision, can approach the reasoning reliability of much larger evaluators on focused tasks, with performance saturating around one billion parameters (Chopra et al., 2025). This is direct evidence that the capability gap is narrowest, and the case for external memory strongest, precisely in the small-model range the present study targets.

### 2.3.3 Techniques for On-Device Deployment

On the device itself, retrieval and compression must respect a fixed hardware budget, and the literature converges on retrieval-first designs that keep the generative model small while delegating knowledge to an external store. A common pattern pairs an on-device small model with retrieval over local documents, so that the generative component stays compact and the memory grows independently (Patra et al., 2026). Reviews of on-device deployment emphasise that this is realised through inference-time systems, lightweight runtimes, hardware-aware kernels, and quantised key-value caches, rather than through larger models, and that energy and intermittent connectivity, not raw accuracy, are usually the binding constraints (Shafin et al., 2026). Working systems confirm the viability of the approach: a fully offline conversational assistant built on a locally deployed small model, with on-device semantic validation of its own responses, has been shown to reach accuracy within roughly three percentage points of cloud services while keeping all data on the device and maintaining low latency (Anusha et al., 2026). The personalised knowledge refinement framework discussed above is also explicitly deployment-oriented, packaging its distilled knowledge for cloud-free, on-device inference under strict memory and latency limits (Wang et al., 2026).

Retrieval on a device can also be made cheap by combining two inexpensive rankings instead of one costly one. Ranking by word match with the BM25 function needs only an inverted index (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009), ranking by meaning needs only a comparison of embedding vectors (Karpukhin et al., 2020), and reciprocal rank fusion merges the two lists without any training (Cormack et al., 2009). Taken together, this body of work establishes that the techniques exist and are individually effective on a device; what it does not provide, and what motivated the present study, is a measured account of how much of the small-versus-large capability gap a memory engine actually closes, by task type, under explicit token and latency accounting on consumer hardware.

## 2.4 Related Works

This section examines specific systems that implement memory for language models, from operating-system inspired context management to brain-inspired associative retrieval and production memory layers. It then examines three systems that Burrow is compared with or built to work beside: a vector database, a personal knowledge vault, and a cloud coding assistant. Table 2.1 summarises the comparison.

### 2.4.1 MemGPT

Packer et al. (2023) framed the memory problem in operating-system terms. The model's limited context window is treated as fast main memory, an external store as disk, and the system pages information in and out as the conversation demands, allowing a fixed-context model to sustain interactions far longer than its window. The framing is influential because it makes explicit that what the model reads at each step is a policy decision, not a given, which is exactly the decision this study's token budgeting mechanism controls. MemGPT manages context length, however; it does not target capability gain, and it does not account for token cost or device latency. Figure 2.1 illustrates the two-tier architecture.

### 2.4.2 Generative Agents

Park et al. (2023) gave simulated agents a memory stream of timestamped observations and retrieved from it by combining three scores: relevance to the current situation, recency, and importance. Their generative agents produced believable long-running behaviour in a simulated town, and their retrieval score shaped the retrieval policy first proposed for this study. The system was designed for simulation rather than question answering, and its memory records are verbose natural-language entries, which makes it an instructive design source but not an answer to the efficiency questions a device imposes. Figure 2.2 shows the simulated town in which the agents live, work, and remember.

### 2.4.3 MemoryBank

Zhong et al. (2024) added a model of forgetting. MemoryBank strengthens or decays stored memories over time in the spirit of the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve, so that what the system retains reflects both how often a memory is used and how old it is. The contribution is a principled answer to what should be kept, complementing the question of what should be retrieved. Like the systems above, MemoryBank is evaluated on the quality of long-term companionship behaviour rather than on capability gain, token cost, or latency. Figure 2.3 reproduces the framework figure from the project's repository, showing the memory bank, the forgetting curve, and retrieval into conversation.

### 2.4.4 HippoRAG

Plain similarity search retrieves passages that resemble the question, but some questions require connecting facts that do not resemble the question at all. Cognitive science offers a model for this in spreading activation, where recalling one concept activates linked concepts (Collins & Loftus, 1975), and in the hippocampal indexing theory, where a compact index links the distributed traces of an experience so that touching one part can reactivate the whole (Teyler & DiScenna, 1986). Gutiérrez et al. (2024) operationalised these ideas in HippoRAG: a language model extracts entities from stored passages into a knowledge graph that acts as the index, and at question time the entities in the query seed a Personalized PageRank walk over the graph, spreading relevance to connected passages, including multi-hop neighbours that similarity search misses. Figure 2.4, reproduced from the project's repository, illustrates the pipeline. HippoRAG outperformed strong retrieval baselines on multi-hop question answering by up to twenty percent while being many times cheaper and faster than iterative retrieval methods. Its relevance to this study is twofold: it demonstrates the value of associative structure in memory, and its computational profile, graph construction by a language model and a graph walk per query, illustrates exactly the kind of cost that must be justified against a device budget. This is why Burrow follows the links that already exist between a person's files instead of asking a language model to build a graph.

### 2.4.5 Mem0

Production systems have begun packaging these ideas. Mem0 extracts and consolidates salient facts from conversations into a managed store and reports large token savings relative to feeding a model its full history (Chhikara et al., 2025). Its existence is evidence that the engineering direction is commercially credible. As a cloud service, however, it does not answer the on-device question, and like the research systems it does not measure gap closing per task type. Figure 2.5, drawn from the project's documentation, shows where the memory layer sits in an agent stack, reading at question time and writing after each response.

### 2.4.6 Chroma

The most common way to give a language model external knowledge today is a vector database placed in a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline. Chroma is a widely used open-source example. Documents are cut into pieces, each piece is stored in a collection with an identifier, an embedding vector, optional metadata, and its text, and at question time the pieces nearest to the question are pasted into the prompt (Chroma, 2026). Chroma runs as an embedded library on one machine or as the distributed service shown in Figure 2.6, so it suits a laptop as well as a server. It is the baseline of this study because it represents what a developer would build without a memory engine. A vector database is a store, however, and not an engine: it returns the nearest pieces to every question, whether or not the question needs them, in the same quantity for every model, and it does not check what the model then says.

### 2.4.7 Obsidian

Obsidian is a note-taking application built on a simple idea of ownership. A vault is an ordinary folder of plain Markdown files on the person's own disk, and the application adds links between notes on top of those files without locking them into a database (Obsidian, 2025a). Its graph view, shown in Figure 2.7, draws each note as a circle and each link as a line, so that the structure of a person's knowledge becomes visible (Obsidian, 2025b). Burrow adopts the same form of memory: its vault is a folder of Markdown notes that Obsidian can open, and the links between notes are the links its retrieval follows. Obsidian itself is a tool for a person to write and read. It does not select what a language model should read from the vault, fit that selection to the model, or measure what the model gains from it.

!fig figures/related/fig2-7-obsidian.png | Figure 2.7: The graph view of an Obsidian vault. Source: Obsidian (2025b), https://help.obsidian.md/plugins/graph. | 4.8

### 2.4.8 Claude Code and the Model Context Protocol

Claude Code is a coding assistant from Anthropic that reads a codebase, edits files, and runs commands on the developer's behalf (Anthropic, 2026a). It keeps a project memory in plain files: instructions the developer writes in a file named `CLAUDE.md`, and notes the assistant accumulates by itself across sessions (Anthropic, 2026b). It reaches outside tools and data through the Model Context Protocol, an open standard that lets any AI application talk to any data source in one common way (Model Context Protocol, 2026), as Figure 2.8 shows. Claude Code is the strongest form of the cloud side of this study: a large model with a memory of the project. Its model runs in the cloud, so the questions and the files it reads leave the device, and its memory serves a large model rather than closing the gap for a small one. Burrow was built to work beside it. Burrow's own Model Context Protocol server lets Claude Code, or any other tool that speaks the protocol, use the same vault.

## 2.5 Gaps in Related Works

The reviewed literature establishes each ingredient separately. Retrieval can substitute for model scale on knowledge-heavy tasks (Borgeaud et al., 2022; Izacard et al., 2023; Lewis et al., 2020), and it helps most on exactly the long-tail knowledge where small models fail (Mallen et al., 2023). Memory systems can accumulate, organise, score, and forget information across long interactions (Chhikara et al., 2025; Gutiérrez et al., 2024; Packer et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024). Text supplied to a model can be heavily compressed with little loss (Chevalier et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2023). Benchmarks exist that expose how poorly current systems remember long interactions (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Vector databases, personal vaults, and cloud assistants with project memory are in daily use (Anthropic, 2026b; Chroma, 2026; Obsidian, 2025a).

What is missing is the combination and the measurement. No existing work assembles these ingredients into a memory engine beside a frozen small model on a consumer device and then answers the deployment question directly: what fraction of the small-versus-large capability gap does the engine close, separately for knowledge-bound and reasoning-bound tasks, and at what cost in tokens and seconds on the device? Existing memory systems are evaluated on recall or task success, not on gap closing; compression is evaluated on prompts, not on persistent stores; and no study reports honest cost accounting on consumer hardware against both a plain vector database and a cloud model. This intersection, gap closing by task type under explicit token and latency budgets on a device, defines the gap this study addressed.

## 2.6 Conceptual Framework

Figure 2.9 presents the conceptual framework that structured the study. The independent variable is the configuration of the memory engine: the Decide step that chooses whether a question needs the vault and whether to search or to follow links, the Fit step that sets the token budget limiting how much memory the model reads, the Check step that verifies the sources an answer cites, the Learn step that turns a conversation into stored notes, and the skills the engine hands to the model with the question. The process is question answering on the device: the engine recalls relevant excerpts, assembles them within the budget, and the frozen on-device model generates an answer from the question plus the assembled memory. The dependent variables are the fraction of the small-versus-large capability gap closed, the accuracy gained per token of memory supplied, and the end-to-end latency on the target device. Task type, knowledge-bound versus reasoning-bound, moderates the relationship: the study predicted substantial gap closing on knowledge-bound questions and little on reasoning-bound questions. The language model itself was held constant throughout, weights frozen, so that every measured gain is attributable to the memory engine alone.

# Chapter 1:  Introduction

## 1.1 Background Information

Language models have become the foundation of modern conversational software, powering assistants that answer questions, summarise documents, and help with everyday tasks. The most capable of these models contain tens or hundreds of billions of parameters and run as cloud services on specialised hardware. Their capability comes at a cost: every query travels over a network, personal data leaves the user's device, the service fails without connectivity, and each request carries a price. Empirical work on scaling has shown that capability grows predictably with model size and training data (Kaplan et al., 2020), which is precisely why the strongest models are also the largest and the least practical to run locally.

A parallel movement has formed around small language models that run directly on consumer hardware such as laptops and phones. Small models such as Qwen2.5 (Yang et al., 2024), Llama 3.2 (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Phi-3 (Abdin et al., 2024), and the on-device foundation models that Apple now ships on its consumer chips (Gunter et al., 2024) demonstrate that useful language ability fits comfortably in the memory of an ordinary computer. The open-weight families released in 2026, Gemma 4 (Gemma Team, 2026) and Qwen3.5 (Qwen Team, 2026), continue this direction with models sized for a laptop. On-device deployment offers real advantages: answers are generated locally with low latency, private data never leaves the machine, the assistant keeps working offline, and the marginal cost of a query is zero. A survey of the field confirms both the rapid progress of small models and their persistent weaknesses relative to their larger counterparts (Lu et al., 2024).

The most consistent of those weaknesses is factual knowledge. A small model simply has not memorised as many facts as a large one. Mallen et al. (2023) showed this directly: language models struggle with less popular, long-tail factual knowledge, retrieval of external text helps significantly in exactly those cases, and making models bigger mainly improves recall of popular facts while barely improving the long tail. This finding suggests a practical opportunity. If much of the gap between small and large models is missing knowledge rather than missing reasoning ability, then supplying the right knowledge at the right time, from outside the model, might recover a meaningful share of the gap without touching the model at all.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

Users who want a private, offline, low-cost assistant must accept a small on-device model, and with it a marked drop in capability, most visibly on questions that require factual knowledge the small model has not memorised. The two standard remedies do not fit the on-device setting. Replacing the small model with a larger one defeats the purpose, because the larger model no longer fits on the device. Fine-tuning the small model on new knowledge requires compute that ordinary users do not have, must be repeated whenever knowledge changes, and risks degrading the model's existing abilities.

Retrieval-augmented approaches offer a third path: leave the model frozen and supply relevant text at question time. The research literature has established the ingredients separately. Retrieval can substitute for model scale on knowledge-heavy tasks (Borgeaud et al., 2022; Izacard et al., 2023; Lewis et al., 2020). Memory systems can accumulate and organise information across long interactions (Gutiérrez et al., 2024; Packer et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024). Prompts can be compressed so that less text carries the same meaning (Jiang et al., 2023). What no existing work provides is a measured answer to the question that matters for on-device deployment: when these ingredients are combined into a memory engine beside a frozen small model on a real consumer device, what fraction of the small-versus-large capability gap is actually recovered, on which kinds of tasks, and at what cost in tokens and latency? Without that answer, designers of on-device assistants cannot know whether memory engineering is a worthwhile substitute for model scale, or where its limits lie.

Answering that question matters because on-device assistants are fast becoming the default way millions of users reach a language model privately, offline, and at no recurring cost, yet there is still no measured account of how far memory engineering can narrow the capability gap that separates these small models from their cloud counterparts. Until the trade-offs are quantified, designers of on-device assistants are forced to choose between privacy and capability without evidence, and the field cannot say whether memory engineering is a genuine substitute for model scale or only a partial remedy. Producing that evidence is therefore a prerequisite for building on-device assistants that are at once private, low-cost, and capable.

The specific solution this study developed is Burrow, a lightweight external memory engine that sits beside a frozen on-device language model on a consumer laptop. Burrow addresses the problem by giving the on-device model access to an external, persistent memory rather than larger weights. The memory is a vault: a folder of the person's own notes, documents, and code. When a question arrives, the engine decides whether the vault is needed, retrieves the most relevant passages, and packs them into a token budget fitted to the model, while the model's parameters are never changed. After a conversation it proposes compact notes of what the person decided and writes them only with consent. The study then quantified how much of the small-versus-large capability gap this memory recovers, separately for knowledge-bound and reasoning-bound tasks, and at what cost in tokens and latency, thereby answering both what the problem is and how memory engineering can address it on a real device.

## 1.3 Objectives

This section states the general objective of the study and breaks it down into the specific objectives that guided the work.

### 1.3.1 General Objective

The general objective of this study was to design, develop, and validate a lightweight memory engine for a frozen small language model on a consumer laptop, in order to quantify how much of the capability gap between small on-device and large cloud language models such a memory engine can close, by task type, under explicit token and latency accounting.

### 1.3.2 Specific Objectives

i) To investigate the capability gaps between large, small, and on-device language models, together with existing memory, retrieval, and compression techniques and the interaction between a model's internal (parametric) and external (retrieved) knowledge, so as to establish their applicability and limitations in the on-device setting.
ii) To design a lightweight memory engine for a frozen small language model, comprising a write policy that distils interactions into compact stored notes, a retrieval policy that ranks stored passages by word match and by meaning and follows the links between files, and a token budgeting mechanism that controls how much memory the model reads.
iii) To develop the designed memory engine in Swift on an Apple-silicon consumer laptop with no dedicated graphics card, using an embedded database as the index of the memory store.
iv) To evaluate the implemented engine experimentally by measuring the fraction of the capability gap between a small local model and a large cloud model that the engine closes, separately for knowledge-bound and reasoning-bound tasks, together with the token cost and latency of every answer.
v) To determine the token budget that maximises the fraction of the capability gap closed per token, so that the engine can be configured for the best accuracy-to-cost trade-off on a resource-constrained device.

## 1.4 Research Questions

i) What memory, retrieval, and compression techniques exist for language models, and what are their limitations in the on-device setting?
ii) How much of the capability gap between a small on-device language model and a large cloud language model can a frozen-weights memory engine close?
iii) Does the memory engine help more on knowledge-bound questions than on reasoning-bound questions?
iv) What context token budget maximises the fraction of the capability gap closed per token, and how does gap closure scale as the budget increases?
v) What is the cost of the memory engine in tokens and latency when measured on a consumer laptop?

## 1.5 Justification

The study matters for three audiences. For users, especially in settings where connectivity is unreliable or data privacy is a serious concern, a more capable on-device assistant means useful AI that works offline, keeps personal data local, and costs nothing per query. For developers of on-device assistants, the study provides what the literature currently lacks: a measured, component-by-component account of how far memory engineering can substitute for model scale, allowing informed decisions about where to invest effort. For the research community, the study contributes an evaluation method, the gap-closed measure reported per task type with honest token and latency accounting on consumer hardware, that future memory systems can be compared against.

## 1.6 Scope

The deployment target was a consumer laptop, an Apple MacBook Pro with an M5 Pro chip, no dedicated graphics card, and 24 gigabytes of shared memory. This machine was itself the on-device platform on which the engine ran and on which all latency was measured. The on-device models studied were open-weight models that run locally on the target machine through Ollama: Gemma 4 12B as the main model and Qwen3.5 4B as the small model. The large reference model, Claude, was accessed through a cloud API and never ran locally; it served only as the upper anchor against which the gap was measured. Evaluation used questions in English of two kinds, so that knowledge-bound and reasoning-bound performance could be separated. The knowledge-bound questions were single-fact and multi-fact questions on three curated subjects that the on-device model cannot know well. The reasoning-bound questions were mathematical reasoning questions from an established public benchmark. The system delivered is the memory engine and its evaluation harness, together with the surfaces through which a person or another tool uses the engine: a command-line tool, a native Mac application, a terminal chat, and a Model Context Protocol server. The surfaces were added after the Sprint II review of 5 November 2026, which asked for something a person can see and use.

## 1.7 Limitations and Delimitations

### 1.7.1 Limitations

The study operated under constraints that shape how the findings should be interpreted. First, latency measured on one consumer laptop may differ on other machines, so the device results are indicative rather than universal. Second, the large reference model is a hosted service whose behaviour may change over time, which can affect the ceiling used to calculate the capability gap. Third, the evaluation questions are controlled approximations of real assistant use, and the questions on the curated subjects were written by a language model from the source documents rather than by people, so the results may not transfer equally to every live deployment. Fourth, the study was limited by the cost and time required to test memory on many models, devices, languages, and modalities within one undergraduate project.

### 1.7.2 Delimitations

The delimitations respond directly to those constraints. To address the hardware limitation, the study deliberately fixed one consumer laptop as the target device and reports the full hardware specification and the seconds taken by every answer, so that the measurements can be compared with later devices. To address ceiling instability, the study pinned and logged one reference-model version for the whole experiment instead of changing cloud models during evaluation. To address generalisability, the study used three unrelated subjects and one public benchmark, and reports results separately for single-fact, multi-fact, and mathematical reasoning tasks rather than presenting one broad score. To keep the project focused, the study did not train or fine-tune any language model, did not port the engine to mobile phones, and did not evaluate multilingual or multimodal memory. The graph-based associative retrieval layer that the proposal held back as an optional extension was built in a light form, as a walk over the links that already exist between files, because this form needs no language model to construct a graph and so fits the device budget.

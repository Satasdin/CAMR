# Chapter 1:  Introduction

## 1.1 Background Information

Language models have become the foundation of modern conversational software, powering assistants that answer questions, summarise documents, and help with everyday tasks. The most capable of these models are served from the cloud on specialised hardware. Their capability comes at a cost: every query travels over a network, personal data leaves the user's device, and each request carries a price. Z. Zhang et al. (2024) analysed real ChatGPT conversations and interviewed 19 users, and found that people constantly trade privacy against usefulness. Empirical work on scaling has shown that the loss of a language model falls predictably with its size, training data, and compute (Kaplan et al., 2020), which is precisely why the strongest models are also the largest and the least practical to run locally.

A parallel movement has formed around small language models that run directly on consumer hardware such as laptops and phones. Open-weight families such as Qwen2.5 (Yang et al., 2024), Llama 3 (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Phi-3 (Abdin et al., 2024), and the 2026 releases Gemma 4 (Gemma Team, 2026) and Qwen3.5 (Qwen Team, 2026) include models that fit in the memory of an ordinary computer, and Apple now ships a foundation model on its own devices (Gunter et al., 2024). J. Xu et al. (2024) named lower latency, data that stays on the device, and personalisation among the reasons for running a model locally. Lu et al. (2024) measured 70 open models of up to five billion parameters and reported both what they can do and what they cost to run on a device.

The most consistent weakness of these models is factual knowledge. Mallen et al. (2023) showed that language models struggle with less popular facts, that retrieval of external text helps significantly in exactly those cases, and that making models bigger barely improves the long tail. Kandpal et al. (2023) explained why: a model's accuracy on a factual question depends on how many training documents mention the fact. A person's own notes, documents, and code are the extreme case, because a public model is unlikely to have seen them in training. This finding suggests a practical opportunity. If much of the gap between small and large models is missing knowledge rather than missing reasoning ability, then supplying the right knowledge at the right time, from outside the model, might recover a meaningful share of the gap without touching the model at all.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

A person who wants a private, offline, low-cost assistant should be able to run a language model on their own machine and rely on it as they would rely on a cloud service. In practice that person must accept a small on-device model, and with it a marked drop in capability, most visibly on questions that require facts the model has not memorised. The two standard remedies do not fit the on-device setting. A larger model no longer fits in the memory of the device, and fine-tuning the small model needs extensive resources (Soudani et al., 2024) and must be repeated whenever the knowledge changes.

Retrieval offers a third path: leave the model frozen and supply relevant text at question time. The literature has established the ingredients separately. Retrieval can substitute for model scale on knowledge-heavy tasks (Borgeaud et al., 2022; Izacard et al., 2023; Lewis et al., 2020), memory systems can accumulate information across long interactions (Gutiérrez et al., 2024; Packer et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024), and prompts can be compressed so that less text carries the same meaning (Jiang et al., 2023). Retrieved text can also lower accuracy when it is irrelevant (Shi et al., 2023; Yoran et al., 2024), so a memory beside a small model must decide when to supply text and how much. The nearest published measure, in which a small on-device model recovered 87% of a cloud model's performance, depended on working with the cloud model throughout (Narayan et al., 2025). No existing work reports what fraction of the gap a memory engine beside a frozen small model closes on a real consumer device, on which kinds of tasks, and at what cost in tokens and latency.

Without that answer, designers of on-device assistants are forced to choose between privacy and capability without evidence, and the field cannot say whether memory engineering is a genuine substitute for model scale or only a partial remedy.

The specific solution this study developed is Burrow, a lightweight external memory engine that sits beside a frozen on-device language model on a consumer laptop. Burrow addresses the problem by giving the model an external, persistent memory rather than larger weights. It keeps a vault of the person's own notes, documents, and code, decides for each question whether that memory is needed, retrieves the most relevant passages, fits them to the amount the model can read well, and proposes compact notes for the person to keep, while the model's parameters are never changed. The study then measured how much of the capability gap this memory closed, separately for knowledge-bound and reasoning-bound tasks, and at what cost in tokens and latency.

## 1.3 Objectives

This section states the general objective of the study and breaks it down into the specific objectives that guided the work.

### 1.3.1 General Objective

The general objective of this study was to design, develop, and validate a lightweight memory engine for a frozen small language model on a consumer laptop, in order to quantify how much of the capability gap between small on-device and large cloud language models such a memory engine can close, by task type, under explicit token and latency accounting.

### 1.3.2 Specific Objectives

i) To investigate the capability gaps between large, small, and on-device language models, together with existing memory, retrieval, and compression techniques and the interaction between a model's internal (parametric) and external (retrieved) knowledge, so as to establish their applicability and limitations in the on-device setting.
ii) To design a lightweight memory engine for a frozen small language model, comprising a write policy that distils interactions into compact stored notes, a retrieval policy that ranks notes by similarity, recency, and importance, and a token budgeting mechanism that controls how much memory the model reads.
iii) To develop the designed memory engine on a consumer laptop with no dedicated graphics hardware, using an embedded database as the memory store.
iv) To evaluate the implemented engine experimentally by measuring the fraction of the capability gap between a small local model and a large cloud model that the engine closes, separately for knowledge-bound and reasoning-bound tasks, together with the token cost and latency of every answer.
v) To determine the token budget that maximises the fraction of the capability gap closed per token, so that the engine can be configured for the best accuracy-to-cost trade-off on a resource-constrained device.

## 1.4 Research Questions

i) What are the capability gaps between large, small, and on-device language models, and how applicable and limited are existing memory, retrieval, and compression techniques in the on-device setting, given the interaction between a model's internal and retrieved knowledge?
ii) How can a lightweight memory engine for a frozen small language model be designed, in terms of its write policy, its retrieval policy, and its token budgeting mechanism?
iii) How can the designed memory engine be developed on a consumer laptop with no dedicated graphics hardware, using an embedded database as the memory store?
iv) What fraction of the capability gap between a small local model and a large cloud model does the engine close on knowledge-bound and on reasoning-bound tasks, and at what cost in tokens and latency?
v) What token budget maximises the fraction of the capability gap closed per token, and how does gap closure change as the budget increases?

## 1.5 Justification

On-device models are now capable enough to be useful, yet they remain weakest on the knowledge that people most want to keep private, and no study has measured how far an external memory narrows that gap on a consumer device. The study matters for three audiences. For users who handle private material, a more capable on-device assistant keeps that material on their own machine. Mireshghallah et al. (2024) found personally identifiable information in 16% of code-editing conversations with commercial chat models, and in Kenya personal data is protected by statute (Data Protection Act, 2019), with data and AI sovereignty reflected in the national AI strategy (Ogonjo, 2025). For developers of on-device assistants, the study provides a measured account of how far memory engineering can stand in for model scale, allowing informed decisions about where to invest effort. For the research community, the study contributes an evaluation method, the gap-closed measure reported per task type with token and latency accounting on consumer hardware, against which later memory systems can be compared. This work helps people who need a private assistant by giving a small local model access to what they know, while advancing research on memory for on-device language models.

## 1.6 Scope

The deployment target was one consumer laptop, an Apple MacBook Pro with an M5 Pro chip, 24 gigabytes of shared memory, and no dedicated graphics card; this machine was itself the platform on which the engine ran and on which all latency was measured. The on-device models were open-weight models run locally through Ollama, with Gemma 4 12B as the main model and Qwen3.5 4B as the small model. The large reference model, Claude (Anthropic, 2026c), was reached through a cloud API, never ran locally, and served only as the upper anchor against which the gap was measured. Evaluation used 170 English questions: 120 knowledge questions on three curated subjects, with single-fact and multi-fact forms, and 50 mathematical reasoning questions from a public benchmark, so that knowledge-bound and reasoning-bound performance could be separated. The system delivered was the memory engine, its evaluation harness, and four ways to use the engine: a command-line tool, a native Mac application, a terminal chat, and a Model Context Protocol server. The work ran from the approved proposal of June 2026 to the final defence of November 2026, and it did not cover mobile phones, languages other than English, images or audio, or use by several people at once.

## 1.7 Limitations and Delimitations

### 1.7.1 Limitations

The study operated under constraints that shape how the findings should be interpreted. First, latency measured on one consumer laptop may differ on other machines, so the device results are indicative rather than universal. Second, the large reference model is a hosted service whose behaviour may change over time, which can affect the ceiling used to calculate the capability gap. Third, the questions on the curated subjects were written from the source documents by Claude Code agents and not by people, so the results may not transfer equally to every live use. Fourth, the study was limited by the cost and time required to test memory on many models, devices, languages, and modalities within one undergraduate project.

### 1.7.2 Delimitations

The delimitations respond directly to those constraints. To address the hardware limitation, the study deliberately fixed one consumer laptop as the target device and reported the full hardware specification and the seconds taken by every answer. To address ceiling instability, the study pinned and logged one version of the cloud model for the whole experiment. To address the generality of the questions, the study used three unrelated subjects and one public benchmark, and reported single-fact, multi-fact, and mathematical reasoning results separately rather than one broad score. To address the limit of cost and time, the study tested two on-device models on one device in one language, did not train or fine-tune any language model, did not port the engine to mobile phones, and did not evaluate multilingual or multimodal memory. For the same reason, the graph-based retrieval layer that the proposal held back as optional was built in a light form, as a walk over the links that already exist between files.

name: writing
label: Writing
about: Clear, honest writing for pages, answers and reports: plain words, short sentences, and no invented claims.
---
RULE Write the point first. The reader should know what you are saying from the first sentence, not the last.
RULE Use plain words: use, not utilise; help, not facilitate; about, not approximately. If a word needs explaining, find another or explain it once.
RULE Keep sentences short. If a sentence has two ideas, make it two sentences.
RULE Say who does what: "The team fixed it", not "It was fixed". The active voice is shorter and clearer.
RULE Use specific facts instead of adjectives. "Open until 9" says more than "convenient hours". Only use a fact you were given.
RULE Never invent a claim to fill space. No "award-winning", "best in town", "since 1990", testimonials or statistics unless you were told them.
RULE A tagline must be true and specific, or left out. A page without a tagline is fine; a page with a made-up promise is not.
RULE If you do not know something, say so and say what you would need. "I don't have the phone number" is a good sentence.
RULE Say how sure you are when it matters, and separate what you measured from what you think.
RULE Write for the person reading, in their words, about what they want to do, not about how the system works.
RULE Headings should tell the story on their own. Someone skimming only the headings should get the point.
RULE Cut words that add nothing: very, really, simply, just, in order to, please note that.
RULE Use the same word for the same thing throughout. Changing it for variety makes readers think it is a different thing.
RULE When reporting work, say what was done, what was checked, and what was not done. Do not call something finished if part of it failed.
RULE Numbers need context: 29 of 73 says more than 29, and "up from 11" says more still. Give the unit and the conditions.
RULE Read it aloud. If you run out of breath or stumble, rewrite it.
CORRECTION Wrong: "Where great taste meets warm hospitality." Criticism: nobody told you that, and it could be anyone. Right: say what it is and where, using facts you were given. Why: an invented line sounds like every other business.
CORRECTION Wrong: "Our award-winning team." Criticism: no award was mentioned. Right: leave it out. Why: a false claim on someone's page is theirs to answer for.
CORRECTION Wrong: "The task is complete" when the test had failed. Criticism: the most important fact was hidden. Right: "I built it, but the test failed on this line." Why: the reader decides what to do next from what you say.
CORRECTION Wrong: "It leverages a robust architecture to facilitate seamless integration." Criticism: it says nothing. Right: "It connects to your calendar." Why: plain words show whether you have something to say.
CORRECTION Wrong: a five-paragraph answer to a yes-or-no question. Criticism: the answer was buried. Right: answer first, then give only the reason that matters. Why: respect the reader's time.
CORRECTION Wrong: "Unfortunately, an error has occurred." Criticism: no cause and no next step. Right: "The file could not be saved because the disk is full. Free some space and try again." Why: an error message should say what happened and what to do.
CORRECTION Wrong: described the system's inner parts to someone who asked what it does. Criticism: they asked what it does for them. Right: start with what they can now do, and explain the parts only if asked. Why: explanation starts from the reader's question.
CORRECTION Wrong: called the same thing a room, a note and a chunk in one page. Criticism: the reader thought they were three things. Right: pick one word, and use the common term if there is one. Why: invented words cost the reader attention.
